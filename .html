And we zoomed into one town

Tirupur, Tamil Nadu

90% of everything India exports in knitwear. Your cotton T-shirt probably passed through it.

The people

6 lakh workers, 65% of them women from nearby villages.

Why it's the perfect case

Every stage — and every litre of waste water — sits in a few square kilometres. A court has already sued its dye houses over the Noyyal river.

💡Remember one number: 45 million jobs. You can't close this industry down — so the only option is to clean it up. And if you can't fix it in Tirupur, you can't fix it anywhere.
Section 1 · The company we studied

Digsel — and the gap at the heart of it

A menswear brand in Tirupur, under the parent company Selvas Garments. It works with 45+ small units to make its clothes.

What Digsel controls

The choices

Which cloth it buys. Whether leftover fabric gets reused. Which units it gives work to — and what it demands of them.

What it does not control

The dyeing

Dyeing and finishing are sent out to specialist units. Digsel doesn't own the boiler, doesn't own the water, doesn't own the machine that pollutes.

📌This is the whole problem in miniature. The brand takes the blame; someone else runs the boiler. So money and pressure have to travel down the supply chain, not stop at the brand. Every speaker after me comes back to this.
AAnya GuptaSpeaker 02 · Report §2

The industry is going the wrong way

Making clothes causes 8–10% of the world's greenhouse gases — more than all planes and ships put together.

Chart indexed so 2020 = 100. Sources: Leal Filho et al. (2022); H&M Foundation (2026).

1 · the rules are tightening

New laws in America and Europe force big firms to report emissions and check suppliers. India too — SEBI makes the top 1,000 listed firms report, suppliers included. Fall behind and buyers drop you.
(transition risk)

2 · water and weather

Dyeing causes up to 20% of the world's industrial water pollution — and needs clean water to work. Droughts squeeze both ends.
(physical risk)

3 · losing trust

Fake green claims and poor working conditions. Customers and regulators now actually check, so getting caught costs sales.
(reputational risk)

AbAbhirami P RSpeaker 03 · Report §4

Two jobs: stop it getting worse, and survive what's here

Mitigation — stop making it worse
Cleaner electricity. Rooftop solar, or buy wind power from outside. Easiest, and Tamil Nadu has the sun and wind.
Cleaner heat — the big one. Dyeing needs steam, today from coal or gas. Switch to crop-waste boilers, heat pumps, solar water heating, and reuse the hot water you already make. This is the 55%.
Waste less. Machines that dye with far less water, and getting the colour right first time. Cheapest of the lot.
Better cloth. Recycled polyester and cotton, and reusing fabric scraps. New polyester is what's pushing emissions up.
Adaptation — survive what's already here
Water. Tirupur's dye houses already recycle all their water — a court ordered it after the Noyyal river was polluted. The new worry is whether it holds if the rains keep failing.
People. 6 lakh workers, 65% women from villages, in sheds that already get dangerously hot. Insulate roofs, add coolers, give water breaks, move hot jobs off the afternoon.
Buildings and suppliers. One flood stops the whole town. Raise the electrics, insure, and line up backup suppliers before trouble hits.
⚡Two traps to avoid. You cannot fix steam with a solar panel — clean electricity leaves the harder half untouched. And in a drought, that expensive water-recycling plant stops being a cost and becomes the only reason the factory is still open.

SSwastik JenaSpeaker 04 · Report §3

The pollution is here. The money goes there.

Where the pollution is

Small dye houses

Thousands of tiny units. No credit rating, thin accounts, no environment officer, no records — and nothing left to offer the bank as security. This is roughly 55% of the industry's carbon.

Where the money goes

Big listed mills

Rated, audited, already publishing environmental reports, big enough for the paperwork. In other words, already the easiest people in the industry to lend to.

Three reasons the small ones can't borrow

1 · too slow to repay

The boiler takes 5–7 years to pay for itself — Abhirami's chart. Ordinary loans are for months.

2 · nothing is measured

You can't be rewarded for saving water if nobody ever measured your water.

3 · green isn't much cheaper

Green loans carry only a tiny discount. The real prize is discipline and new investors, not cheap money.

🔗So green finance here isn't charity or ethics. It's plumbing — a pipe between where the pollution is and where the money is.
Section 3 · The toolkit

Five ways to pay for a clean-up

Learn these five now — on the next slide you'll be choosing between them yourselves.

What it's calledWhat it actually is, and who it's forThe catch
Green bondBorrowing from investors for one clearly defined green project — a solar plant, a water-recycling plant. For big companies.Must be a large amount, and you report every year on where the money went.
Sustainability-linked loanA normal loan where the interest rate falls if you hit a green target — say, less water per kilo of cloth. For medium firms that can measure.The target must be honest and an outside auditor has to check it.
Climate fund
(“blended finance”)		Cheap or part-donated money that takes the risk out of brand-new technology nobody has funded before.Very little of it exists, and it is slow to approve.
Supply chain financeA big buyer lets its small suppliers borrow against its own good name, and get paid early. For tiny units with no security.Only works if a big, trusted buyer agrees to join in.
Carbon creditsNot a loan — income. Prove you cut emissions and sell that saving. India's new carbon trading scheme covers textiles.Prices swing, and proving it costs money.
🔑The one idea to hold on to: the catch decides who can use it, not the label. A green bond isn't better than supply chain finance — it's for a completely different borrower.

HHayden BinsonSpeaker 05 · Report §5
Financing one small company is difficult. But several of them can be bunched together and financed as one.
— Operations Director, Digsel (Selvas Garments) · our own interview, Tirupur

The unfair bit: the factories least able to prove anything are exactly the ones where the biggest improvements are possible. So the gap between big and small widens on its own. Here's the structure that closes it.

Who pays
Banks and big buyers — they set your interest rate on proof, not promises
Who checks
An independent auditor. In India SEBI's BRSR rule now makes this compulsory for big listed firms and their suppliers — which is what forces records to exist at all
Who collects
The town's trade association — gathers everyone's data on one template and shares the cost and the know-how
The factory floor
Each unit records its electricity, steam, water, waste and working conditions
🧩Take out the “who collects” layer and the whole thing falls down: one small factory can't produce proof alone, so the bank goes back to demanding property. That is exactly what our interview described — and it's where our main recommendation comes from.
Your turn · call it out

Real, or just good marketing?

Three claims that all sound green. Shout out which ones you'd believe — then I'll show you what a checker would say.

“Our new range uses 30% recycled polyester, checked by an outside agency, and we publish the list of factories we use.”
realA specific number, checked by someone independent, and traceable. All three boxes ticked — this is what an honest claim looks like.
“We are committed to becoming carbon neutral by 2050, using high-quality offsets.”
greenwashing2050 is beyond everyone's career, there's no target for next year, and “offsets” means paying someone else to cut instead of cutting your own. Impressive words, no accountability.
“Our dye house recycles 100% of its water, certified by the state pollution board.”
true — but not an achievementCompletely true. But in Tirupur recycling all your water is the law, not an extra effort. Presenting basic legal compliance as going the extra mile is the sneakiest kind of greenwashing, and the hardest to catch.

Section 7 · Recommendations

Six recommendations — and who has to act

01 · the headline · banks

Fund small factories as a group, not one by one. Match the loan length to the 5–7 year payback. That's the only way they become worth lending to.

02 · government

Make the schemes findable. They exist but nobody knows about them. And keep a “transition” category so half-clean options like crop-waste boilers still qualify.

03 · big buyers

Fund your suppliers, don't just audit them. Inspecting a factory doesn't buy it a boiler. Lend it your credit rating instead.

04 · factories

Measure first, borrow second. And do it with cheap, simple systems built for a 12-machine unit, not for a listed company.

05 · trade associations

Collect the data and buy the checking together. Independent verification is what leaves greenwashing nowhere to hide.

06 · the condition · everyone

A just transition — no small factory, no worker and no supplier left without support. 45 million livelihoods depend on it.

🤝Notice what's missing: a single hero. No one of these five can fix it alone — which is exactly why our headline answer is a shared structure rather than a stronger individual.
